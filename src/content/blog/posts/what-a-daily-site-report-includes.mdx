On a busy site, the daily report is easy to put off. It gets written late, from memory, or not at all. Then a question comes up weeks later about a delay, a delivery or extra work, and the report is the one record everyone wishes they had.

A good report does not need to be long. It needs to be specific, written the same day, and consistent from one day to the next.

## The basics: date, weather and conditions

Start with the date, the site and who wrote the report. Then record the weather and site conditions as they affected the work: rain that stopped a pour, wind that grounded a crane, heat that changed working hours. Note the times. "Rain until 11:00, pour delayed one hour" is far more useful later than "wet morning".

## Who was on site

List the crews and trades on site and roughly how many people from each. Note visitors and inspectors too. This is what shows, later on, whether a trade was there when it should have been.

## Progress by area

Describe what was done, area by area: which level, which room, which part of the structure. Where you can, say how far along each task is. Progress written against locations is easier to compare from day to day than a general summary.

## Deliveries, delays and problems

Record what arrived and what did not. When something was late, say what it held up. Write down problems as they happened: a clash between trades, a missing drawing, damaged materials. Facts and times matter more than opinions.

## Instructions and decisions

If someone gave an instruction on site, record who gave it, what it was and when. Verbal instructions are the ones most often disputed later.

## Photos, with captions

Photos are strong evidence, but only if someone can tell what they show. Note the location and what the photo is meant to show, and keep photos with the report they belong to.

## Write it while the day is fresh

The most important rule is timing. A report written at the end of the day, or first thing the next morning, is more accurate than one rebuilt days later.
